\begin{abstract}
Environmental Information Disclosure is an emerging macroeconomic data
element in China's capital markets. This paper embeds publicly reported
EID scores directly into a Proximal Policy Optimization state space and
tests whether this information improves neural-network structural
stability. Using 59,051 firm-year EID observations from the Hexun EID
Database for Chinese listed companies from 2008 to 2025, with
out-of-sample portfolio evaluation over 2022--2024, the model that
combines EID and regime weighting reduces normalized Frobenius parameter
distance by 36\% relative to baseline PPO when EID is added without
regime conditioning, and by 50\% when both components are included; both
improvements are significant at $p<0.001$. A permutation test confirms
that the improvement reflects EID's firm-level economic content rather
than dimensionality expansion. A regime-only ablation model, which
withholds EID entirely, achieves a statistically insignificant 3.3\%
reduction, isolating parameter stability gains specifically to EID. At a
proportional transaction cost of 0.2\% per turnover unit, the full model
achieves a net Sharpe ratio of 0.58 versus 0.29 for the Markowitz
minimum-variance benchmark. A mediation analysis attributes 28\% of the
stability gain to the reward-variance channel; the remaining share
operates through pathways not yet precisely identified, and causal
interpretation requires a stronger identification design.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Green Finance, Environmental Information
Disclosure, Proximal Policy Optimization, Deep Reinforcement Learning,
Neural Network Stability, Portfolio Optimization, China
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Between 2015 and 2024, Chinese regulators levied environmental
penalties against more than 11,000 A-share listed firms, and the
Hexun EID scoring system publicly rates each company's environmental
governance quality on a 0--100 scale. Despite the scale of this
dataset---59,051 firm-year observations across the full 2008--2025
sample---most dynamic portfolio optimization models treat the
investment universe as defined solely by price and volume
microstructure, ignoring the regulatory-risk signal embedded in
disclosure scores.

Portfolio optimization has evolved from Markowitz's
\citeyearpar{markowitz1952portfolio} mean-variance framework through
multifactor models \citep{famafrench1993common} to machine-learning
approaches. \citet{demiguel2009optimal} show that naive equal-weighting
frequently matches or beats parametric optimizers out-of-sample,
motivating the search for information sets rich enough to deliver
genuine out-of-sample gains. Deep reinforcement learning has emerged
as a natural candidate for dynamic portfolio problems because it does
not require specifying a transition model \citep{deng2017deep,zhang2020},
but existing implementations restrict state spaces to price and volume
microstructure. In parallel, a growing literature documents that
environmental and ESG characteristics carry systematic risk information
\citep{renneboog2008socially,friede2015,bolton2021carbon}: firms with
higher carbon exposure earn positive risk premia \citep{hong2019climate},
and green assets display lower tail risk during regulatory stress events
\citep{pastor2021,pedersen2021}. Yet the mechanism through which
environmental disclosure enters DRL training dynamics has not been
examined. The information-disclosure literature suggests a route: higher
quality disclosure lowers information asymmetry
\citep{verrecchia2001essays,lambert2007accounting}, which reduces the
variance of the reward signal the agent observes. Lower reward variance,
in turn, implies more consistent gradient directions across training
runs --- the quantity measured by normalized Frobenius distance.

The default assumption in DRL portfolio optimization
\citep{schulman2017proximal,mnih2015} is that firm-level environmental
data either get priced in quickly or are redundant with standard
microstructure variables. We challenge this assumption directly. When
EID scores enter the PPO state space alongside price microstructure,
independently trained networks on non-overlapping data subsets converge
to parameter configurations that are 36--50\% closer in normalized
Frobenius norm ($p<0.001$ in both cases). The regime-only ablation
(Model D, no EID) produces a statistically insignificant 3.3\%
reduction under the same training protocol, ruling out dimensionality
and regime conditioning as the source of the improvement.

\paragraph{A note on causal language.}
Throughout this paper, claims are \emph{associational}: models trained
with EID exhibit lower normalized Frobenius distance across training
partitions. The hypothesized causal chain --- EID reduces reward noise,
which lowers gradient variance, which lowers Frobenius distance --- is
theoretically motivated and empirically consistent with the data, but
the observational design does not possess the identification conditions
for a causal proof. We use
``associated with'' and ``consistent with'' rather than ``causes''
throughout, and we identify the additional evidence---quasi-natural
experiment designs exploiting mandatory disclosure policy changes---that
would be needed to establish causality.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The paper's primary contribution is methodological: we establish
normalized Frobenius distance as a diagnostic for state-variable
quality in DRL portfolio optimization, and we introduce a controlled
ablation design --- Model D, which uses regime weighting but withholds
EID --- that isolates precisely what EID adds beyond regime
conditioning. A companion empirical
contribution is the mediation decomposition, which attributes 28\% of
the stability improvement to the reward-variance channel and explicitly
flags the remaining share as an open measurement problem. Translating
the stability gains to practical value, we report net-return
comparisons at three transaction-cost levels against seven benchmarks,
including a static ESG tilt that achieves only 60\% of Model C's
Sharpe ratio despite targeting the same green securities.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}

\subsubsection{EID as Policy-Risk Signal: Motivating Assumption}

\begin{assumption}[EID as Modified Markov Transition Probability]
\label{ass:eid_mdp}
Let the probability of a regulatory policy shock during period $t$
conditional on firm $i$'s EID score $e_{it}$ be:
\begin{equation}
  P(\mathrm{PolicyShock}_t\mid e_{it})=\alpha_0-\alpha_1 e_{it}+\varepsilon_t,
  \label{eq:policy_shock}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_1>0$. Higher disclosure quality then lowers the
probability of an idiosyncratic regulatory shock, which modifies the
MDP state transition distribution and generates a positive value
gradient $\partial V/\partial e_{it}>0$. The gradient operates through
the downside-risk channel: $\partial\,\mathrm{LPM}_{q,i}/\partial
e_{it}<0$ for $q\geq 1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}
Assumption~\ref{ass:eid_mdp} is a \emph{sufficient condition}, not a
proved theorem. The sign constraint $\alpha_1>0$ is directly testable:
Table~\ref{tab:alpha_test} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism_test}) reports
a firm-level regression of realized policy-shock exposure on EID scores,
providing an empirical check. We additionally assume that policy shocks
are on net associated with \emph{negative} firm-level abnormal returns,
consistent with the negative downside-loss estimates in
Table~\ref{tab:alpha_test}; if shocks were predominantly positive --- subsidy announcements, for
example --- the LPM channel would not apply.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Hypothesized mechanism chain.}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Higher EID}
&\Rightarrow \text{lower info asymmetry} \\
&\Rightarrow \text{lower policy-shock variance in rewards} \\
&\Rightarrow \text{lower }\mathrm{Var}(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)) \\
&\Rightarrow \text{lower Frobenius distance.}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
This chain has intermediate-variable predictions tested in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism_test}.
Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} visualizes each link with its corresponding
empirical test annotation.

\begin{proposition}[Unified Regime-Adaptive Green Preference]
\label{prop:regime}
Let $p_t^{\mathrm{bull}} = P(\mathrm{Regime}_t=\mathrm{Bull}\mid
\mathcal{F}_t)$ denote the HMM filtered posterior probability of being
in the bullish regime at time $t$. The regime-adaptive green preference
weight is defined continuously as:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_2(t) = \lambda_2^{\min}
    + (\lambda_2^{\max}-\lambda_2^{\min})\cdot p_t^{\mathrm{bull}},
  \label{eq:adaptive_lambda}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_2^{\min}=0.05$ and $\lambda_2^{\max}=0.15$. In the
limit $p_t^{\mathrm{bull}}\to 1$ (strongly bullish), $\lambda_2\to0.15$;
when $p_t^{\mathrm{bull}}\to 0$ (strongly bearish), $\lambda_2\to0.05$.
This continuous mapping is economically motivated: environmental
preference is strongest when risk budgets are ample and weakest when
capital preservation dominates, with the HMM posterior providing a
smooth, real-time market state signal rather than a sharp binary
classification.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{HMM specification.}
The two-state HMM follows the regime-switching framework of
\citet{hamilton1989new}, using Gaussian emission distributions fitted
to 20-day rolling log-returns and estimated via the Baum--Welch
algorithm with $k$-means initialization. Asset allocation under
regime switching has been extensively studied
\citep{ang2002international}; our contribution is to map the HMM
posterior continuously into the reward weight $\lambda_2(t)$ rather
than using a hard classification rule. The estimated steady-state probabilities
are $\hat\pi_{\mathrm{bull}}\approx0.61$ and
$\hat\pi_{\mathrm{bear}}\approx0.39$, with average regime durations of
approximately 4.2 months (bull) and 2.6 months (bear). The transition
matrix and emission parameters are estimated on training data only
(2016--2021) and held fixed in deployment.

\subsubsection{State Space Construction}

The base state vector at time $t$ is $S_t=\{P_t, I_t, E_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_t=(r_{1t},\ldots,r_{Nt})$: daily returns ($N$ assets).
  \item $I_t=(\mathrm{MA}_{5,t},\mathrm{MA}_{20,t},\mathrm{RSI}_t,
    \mathrm{MACD}_t,\sigma_{it})$: standard technical indicators and
    30-day EWMA volatility ($\lambda_{\mathrm{decay}}=0.94$).
  \item $E_t^{\mathrm{raw}}=(e_{1t}^{\mathrm{raw}},\ldots,e_{Nt}^{\mathrm{raw}})$:
    the official aggregate EID scores carried forward from the most
    recent annual disclosure date $t_{\mathrm{disc}}(y)$.
    If the actual release date is unknown, the conservative deadline
    (April 30, year $y+1$) is used, ensuring strict look-ahead
    prevention.
\end{itemize}
EID scores are monotonically normalized:
$\tilde{e}_{it}=e_{it}^{\mathrm{raw}}/\max_{i,t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}}e_{it}^{\mathrm{raw}}$,
preserving rank and interval ordering. During deployment (2022--2024),
values exceeding the training maximum are clipped:
$\tilde{e}_{it}=\min(e_{it}^{\mathrm{raw}}/\max_{\mathrm{train}},\,1)$.
In our data the deployment maximum (45.0 in 2024, Table~\ref{tab:eid_descriptives})
exceeds the training maximum; fewer than 3\% of out-of-sample firm-year
observations are affected. Results are robust to an expanding-window
maximum normalization (available in the online supplement). Non-EID
features use expanding-window min-max normalization in training and
252-day rolling windows in deployment.

\subsubsection{Reward Function}

\begin{equation}
  R_t = r_p(t) - \lambda_{\mathrm{risk}}\sigma_p(t)
      + \lambda_1\Delta\mathrm{SR}_t
      + \lambda_2(t)\cdot\mathrm{Green}_t,
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $r_p(t)=\sum_i w_{it}r_{it}$,
$\sigma_p(t)=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}w_{it}w_{jt}\mathrm{Cov}(r_{it},r_{jt})}$,
$\Delta\mathrm{SR}_t$ is the change in trailing 252-day Sharpe ratio,
$\mathrm{Green}_t=\sum_i w_{it}e_{it}^{\mathrm{raw}}$, and
$\lambda_2(t)$ follows Proposition~\ref{prop:regime}.
Hyperparameters: $\lambda_{\mathrm{risk}}=2.0$, $\lambda_1=0.5$.

\paragraph{Transaction costs.}
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{TO}_t &= \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N|w_{it}-w_{i,t-1}|,
  \label{eq:turnover} \\
  r_{p,t}^{\mathrm{net}} &= r_{p,t}^{\mathrm{gross}} - c\cdot\mathrm{TO}_t,
  \label{eq:net_return}
\end{align}
with $c\in\{0.001,0.002,0.003\}$ (0.1--0.3\% per unit of turnover).
Net returns are the primary performance measure.

\subsection{PPO Training}

Actor and critic networks share the architecture
$\mathrm{Input}_{d_s}\to\mathrm{ReLU}_{256}\to\mathrm{ReLU}_{256}

clipped objective uses $\varepsilon=0.2$ with GAE advantage estimation
($\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}=0.95$); full hyperparameter

\subsection{Four Model Variants}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Model A --- Baseline PPO: state $\{P_t,I_t\}$; constant $\lambda_2=0.10$.
  \item Model B --- PPO+EID: state $\{P_t,I_t,E_t\}$; constant $\lambda_2=0.10$.
  \item Model C --- PPO+EID+Regime: state $\{P_t,I_t,E_t\}$; adaptive
    $\lambda_2(t)$ via Proposition~\ref{prop:regime}.
  \item Model D --- PPO+Regime without EID (ablation): state $\{P_t,I_t\}$;
    adaptive $\lambda_2(t)$ via Proposition~\ref{prop:regime}. Isolates
    the regime-reward contribution from the EID-signal contribution.
\end{itemize}
Models B vs.\ A: isolates EID signal (reward function held constant).
Models C vs.\ D: isolates EID signal (regime reward held constant).
Models D vs.\ A: isolates regime-reward design (EID held absent).

\subsection{Neural Network Structural Stability}
\label{sec:stability_metric}

\subsubsection{Justification of Frobenius Distance}

We use normalized Frobenius distance between independently trained
networks as a stability metric, motivated by three considerations.

\emph{Theoretical grounding.}
Under mild Lipschitz smoothness of the policy network with constant $L$,
$\|\mathbf{w}_a-\mathbf{w}_b\|_2\leq\delta \Rightarrow
\sup_{s}\mathrm{TV}(\pi_a(\cdot|s),\pi_b(\cdot|s))\leq L\delta$.
Thus parameter proximity upper-bounds policy-distribution divergence.

\emph{Sensitivity to spurious fitting.}
Spurious EID signal would drive independently trained networks toward
different EID-channel weights, increasing Frobenius distance. Persistent
genuine signal would induce consistent gradient directions, reducing it.

\emph{Computation and interpretability.}
No density estimation required; the normalization factor
$\frac{1}{2}(\|\mathbf{w}_a\|_2+\|\mathbf{w}_b\|_2)$ renders the
metric dimensionless and scale-invariant.

\begin{remark}[Limitations]
Lower Frobenius distance is necessary but not sufficient for policy
quality. Two networks can be parameter-close yet both learn wrong
allocation directions. We therefore require three additional checks:
signed positive EID attribution with $w_{\mathrm{EID}}>0$
(Table~\ref{tab:feature_importance}), superior net-of-cost
out-of-sample performance (Table~\ref{tab:net_performance}), and
permutation tests that destroy EID economic content
(Section~\ref{sec:permutation}).
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Metric Definitions}

The normalized distance between two model parameter sets $M_a$, $M_b$:
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{norm}}(M_a,M_b)
    = \frac{\|\mathbf{w}_a-\mathbf{w}_b\|_2}
           {\tfrac{1}{2}(\|\mathbf{w}_a\|_2+\|\mathbf{w}_b\|_2)}.
  \label{eq:frobenius_norm}
\end{equation}
Summary statistics over $K$ partition pairs:
$\bar{D}=\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\!\sum_{i<j}D_{\mathrm{norm}}(M_i,M_j)$;
$D_{\max}=\max_{i,j}D_{\mathrm{norm}}(M_i,M_j)$;
$\mathrm{CV}_D=\mathrm{std}(D)/\bar{D}$.
Statistical inference uses Mann--Whitney $U$ tests, bootstrap
confidence intervals from $B=1{,}000$ resamples of the distance matrix,
and Cohen's $d$ effect sizes.

\subsubsection{Partitioning Strategy}

Three partition dimensions are used. Temporal partitions divide the
data into 4-year rolling windows covering 2016--2019, 2018--2021,
2020--2023, and 2021--2024, yielding six pairs. The market-regime
partition separates bull and bear periods into one pair. The sector
partition separates high-emission from low-emission firms into one pair.
All eight pairs generate one distance each; $\bar{D}$ averages across
all eight.

\section{Data and Sample Construction}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{EID Data}

The primary dataset comprises 59,051 firm-year observations from the
Hexun Environmental Information Disclosure Database, which aggregates
annual corporate environmental reports, sustainability reports, and
regulatory filings for Chinese A-share listed companies into a
composite score from 0 to 100, with typical values in the 0--50 range. This database has been widely used in Chinese environmental disclosure
research \citep{liu2020}. Voluntary environmental disclosure choices
reflect strategic responses to regulatory pressure
\citep{clarkson2011voluntary}, and China's mandatory disclosure
expansion after 2015 created plausibly exogenous variation in EID
availability that could support future causal designs
\citep{cai2016corporate}. The aggregate score is used without
sub-category decomposition, preserving the public information set
available to investors.

\paragraph{Data integrity.}
EID scores enter the trading environment only after their public release
date, using April 30 as a conservative deadline where the precise date
is unknown, preventing look-ahead bias. No imputation, winsorization, or
PCA transformation is applied. Industry-residualized EID is tested as a
robustness check in Section~\ref{sec:industry_neutral}.

\paragraph{2025 coverage.}
The 2025 records document database coverage for the full period
($N=1{,}789$ firms) but contribute to portfolio decisions only if
EID scores were publicly released before the relevant trading date
in the 2022--2024 out-of-sample window.

\subsection{Market Data and Risk-Free Rate}

Daily price and volume data: Chinese Stock Connect, Shanghai and
Shenzhen Stock Exchanges, Bloomberg Terminal, and Wind Information
Services (WIND). Price series are split-, dividend-, and
corporate-action-adjusted. Risk-free rate: 1-year Chinese government
bond yield (China Central Depository and Clearing Company).

\subsection{Sample Selection}

Inclusion criteria require stocks to be continuously listed on Shanghai
or Shenzhen exchanges with daily average volume at or above CNY 1M,
complete price data for 2016--2024, EID scores for at least three
consecutive years, and no bankruptcy or extended suspension. The final
sample contains approximately 1,800 stocks.

\paragraph{Survivorship-bias check.}
To address potential survivorship bias from the continuous-listing
requirement, we construct an alternative unbalanced panel that includes
firms delisted or suspended during 2016--2024, treating each delisting as a forced liquidation at the actual
last-trading-day closing price and redistributing the freed portfolio
weight pro-rata to surviving assets from the next rebalancing date.
The stability finding, a $\bar{D}$ reduction of 32\% for A$\to$B and
46\% for A$\to$C, is robust to this inclusion, though absolute
performance metrics decrease by 0.03--0.06 Sharpe points due to the
added volatility. Full unbalanced panel results are available in the
online supplement.

Training: 2016--2021. Out-of-sample evaluation: 2022--2024.

The contrast between Model D and Model B is the key result. Model D
adds regime-adaptive reward weighting to the baseline but withholds
EID; its mean distance $\bar{D}=0.471$ is statistically
indistinguishable from Model A's $\bar{D}=0.487$ ($p=0.193$,
$d=0.21$). Model B adds only EID, without regime conditioning, and
mean distance falls to $\bar{D}=0.312$---a 36\% reduction that is
highly significant ($p<0.001$, $d=1.84$). The difference in outcomes
between these two single-component models identifies EID, not regime
weighting, as the source of the stability improvement.

The permutation test addresses a specific competing explanation: that
expanding the state space by any variable---regardless of its economic
content---reduces Frobenius distance mechanically. Shuffling EID
labels randomly across firms within each year (preserving the marginal
distribution) restores $\bar{D}$ to $0.479\pm0.031$, statistically
indistinguishable from Model A. The stability gain therefore requires
the genuine firm-level ordering in EID scores, not merely the extra
dimension.

\subsubsection{Mediation Decomposition}

Following the product-of-coefficients approach with bootstrap confidence
intervals \citet{preacher2008}, we test whether reward variance mediates
the EID-stability association:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{SD}(r_t)_{k} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1\overline{\mathrm{EID}}_k
    + \beta_2\mathrm{Regime}_k + u_k, \label{eq:step1}\\
  D_{\mathrm{norm},k} &= \delta_0
    + \delta_1\widehat{\mathrm{SD}(r_t)}_k + v_k. \label{eq:step2}
\end{align}
Here $\Delta\bar{D}\equiv\bar{D}_A-\bar{D}_C=0.487-0.245=0.242$ is the
total stability improvement (positive values indicate Model C is more
stable than Model A). The indirect effect is
$|\hat{\beta}_1|\times\hat{\delta}_1$; absolute values are used because
the mechanism operates via the chain
EID$\uparrow\to\mathrm{SD}\downarrow\to D_{\mathrm{norm}}\downarrow$
(each step negative, their product $(-)\times(-)$ yields a positive
reduction in $D_{\mathrm{norm}}$, consistent with the positive $\Delta\bar{D}$
convention used throughout).

\section{Robustness and Out-of-Distribution Tests}
\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{Permutation Test}
\label{sec:permutation}

Under 500 EID-label shuffles that reassign scores randomly across firms
within each year, $\bar{D}$ reverts to $0.479\pm0.031$, statistically
indistinguishable from Model A and significantly above Model B
($p<0.002$). This rules out the dimensionality expansion explanation.

\subsection{Alternative Stability Metrics}

Three alternative metrics all qualitatively confirm the Frobenius
result. Spectral distance, measured as the eigenvalue difference of
parameter covariance matrices, yields 0.156 for Model C versus 0.289
for Model A. Wasserstein distance yields 0.234 for Model C versus 0.487
for Model A; note that Wasserstein and Frobenius distances are in
different units, so the numerical coincidence of 0.487 under both
metrics for Model A is incidental and does not imply equivalence.
Cosine similarity is 0.82 for Model C versus 0.71 for Model A.

\subsection{Industry-Neutral EID}
\label{sec:industry_neutral}

With industry-residualized EID
($\tilde{e}_{it}^{\mathrm{resid}}=e_{it}^{\mathrm{raw}}-\bar{e}_{g(i),t}$),
Model B achieves $\bar{D}=0.338$ (vs.\ 0.312 for raw EID). Permutation
test: $p<0.01$. Both within-industry quality variation and
cross-industry policy-risk differentiation contribute to stability
benefits.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}

A grid search over $\lambda_2\in[0.01,0.25]$, $\varepsilon\in[0.1,0.3]$,
and $\alpha\in[0.0001,0.001]$ shows that the stability improvement holds
across 95\% of tested parameter combinations. Marginal degradation
appears only near the search boundary at $\lambda_2=0.25$, suggesting
potential overweighting of the green preference beyond that point, and
at $\varepsilon=0.1$, the tightest trust-region constraint tested.

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution: Policy Shock Events}

During environmental regulation announcement windows (2022 ETS
expansion, 2023 water-pollution enforcement), Model C exhibits lower
drawdowns and smaller volatility spikes than Model A, consistent with
the policy-risk filtering mechanism. However, these comparisons are
descriptive: events are not randomized and firm-size and liquidity
confounds are not controlled. A clean causal test would require an exogenous shock to EID information
availability --- staggered mandatory disclosure policies, for instance
--- which is beyond the current design.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

Table~\ref{tab:frobenius} establishes that regime-adaptive reward
weighting alone --- Model D --- does not move the stability needle
($p=0.193$, $d=0.21$), whereas adding EID --- Model B --- reduces
mean distance by 36\% ($p<0.001$, $d=1.84$). The mediation results in
Table~\ref{tab:mediation} trace 28\% of this gain to the
reward-variance channel: EID reduces firm-level downside risk
exposure, which smooths the reward signal, which in turn lowers
gradient variance across training runs. The remaining 72\% operates
through pathways not yet instrumented---likely including direct
informational effects on the state-transition distribution that bypass
the reward function.

For performance, both EID and regime weighting contribute: Model D,
which uses regime weighting without EID, outperforms Model A, while
Model C, which adds EID on top of regime weighting, outperforms Model D
(Table~\ref{tab:net_performance}). Performance gains
\emph{cannot} be causally attributed to any single component without a
controlled experiment.

The framework's practical value is as a \emph{policy-risk filter}: when
market stress windows occur, tilting toward firms with transparent
environmental governance records is associated with lower left-tail
exposure, consistent with pre-disclosed regulatory adaptation capacity.
The result also speaks to the broader question of whether ESG
information earns a return premium or merely reflects risk
\citep{pastor2021,bolton2021carbon}: our evidence suggests a third
channel --- informational quality that improves the training dynamics
of the optimization algorithm itself.

\subsubsection*{Separability of Stability and Performance}
Model D posts a Sharpe ratio of 0.44 at $c=0.002$, outperforming
Model A's 0.36, yet its mean Frobenius distance of $\bar{D}=0.471$
is statistically indistinguishable from Model A at $p=0.193$. Model C
achieves both the highest Sharpe at 0.58 and the lowest Frobenius
distance at $\bar{D}=0.244$. The split outcome---performance without
stability in Model D, both in Model C---reflects two distinct
mechanisms. Regime weighting $\lambda_2(t)$ dynamically scales
environmental preference in the reward function; because it operates
through the reward signal, it can raise returns without systematically
aligning gradient directions across training partitions. EID
introduces a persistent informational signal that influences the
gradient at each training step, producing the parameter consistency
that Frobenius distance captures. That these two mechanisms can be
independently activated is a design feature, not an accidental
outcome: practitioners seeking stability---e.g., as a criterion for
automated retraining triggers---should prioritize the EID component,
while regime weighting adds value as a return-enhancement layer on top
of that foundation.

\subsection{Limitations}

\subsubsection*{Causal Identification}
The full causal chain is not empirically established. The mediation
analysis accounts for only 28\% of the stability improvement through
the reward-variance channel. A cleaner design would be needed for causal claims --- ideally a
difference-in-differences exploiting staggered mandatory disclosure
requirements under China's 2015 Environmental Protection Law
amendments.

\subsubsection*{Transaction Costs and Market Impact}
DRL strategies exhibit higher turnover than passive benchmarks; Model C
turns over approximately 58\% annually. The net Sharpe advantage persists at 0.1--0.3\%
per-turnover costs but would erode at costs above $\approx$0.5\%.
Price-impact costs from large trades are not modeled.

\subsubsection*{Single Market, Single Algorithm}
The EID scoring system, regulatory environment, and market microstructure
are specific to Chinese A-shares. Cross-country generalizability is
untested. Whether similar stability benefits appear in Soft Actor-Critic,
Twin Delayed DDPG, or attention-based architectures remains open.

\subsubsection*{Disclosure Quality versus Environmental Outcomes}
EID scores measure transparency and completeness of reporting, not
actual emissions or regulatory compliance performance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Taken together, the results establish two things that are not obvious
in advance. First, firm-level environmental disclosure quality---a
regulatory-governance signal, not a market variable---carries
information about gradient consistency during DRL training. The
controlled ablation makes this concrete: adding regime-adaptive reward
weighting without EID (Model D) leaves parameter stability
statistically unchanged ($p=0.193$, $d=0.21$), while adding EID
produces a 36--50\% reduction in normalized Frobenius distance
($p<0.001$). The mediation decomposition traces 28\% of this gain to
the reward-variance channel; the majority operates through pathways
not yet instrumentable in an observational design.

Second, stability and performance improvements are separable in the
DRL training process. Model D achieves a higher Sharpe ratio than
Model A (0.44 versus 0.36) through regime-adaptive weighting alone,
even though it fails to improve parameter stability. Model C achieves
both (Sharpe 0.58, Frobenius distance 51\% below Model A) because the
EID and regime-weighting mechanisms operate through different
pathways. The practical implication is specific: EID integration is
the component to prioritize if the goal is a model that is robust to
retraining on new data; regime weighting adds value as a
return-enhancement layer on top of that foundation.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The full causal chain
from EID to gradient consistency is not empirically established: the
mediation analysis accounts for 28\% of the stability improvement
through the reward-variance channel, and testing the remaining 72\%
requires a stronger identification strategy --- ideally a
difference-in-differences design exploiting staggered mandatory
disclosure adoption \citep{cai2016corporate}. Net-return advantages
persist at 0.1--0.3\% per-turnover costs but would erode at higher
levels not modeled here. All results are calibrated to Chinese
A-shares; whether analogous EID-stability relationships hold in other
markets with different disclosure regimes remains an open question.

Future directions include quasi-natural experiments exploiting
staggered disclosure adoption for causal identification, integration
of Scope 1--3 emissions data alongside governance EID scores, testing
the Frobenius diagnostic across Soft Actor-Critic, Twin Delayed DDPG,
and attention-based architectures \citep{lecun2015deep}, and
cross-country validation as ESG disclosure regimes expand globally.
